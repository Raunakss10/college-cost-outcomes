Does the Cost of College Translate into Better Outcomes?

Dhruv Mittal, Raunak Saluja, Thalesh Gupta

Stanford lists a price of $92.9K a year. The average Stanford student pays $12.3K. That is less than the $14.8K a UCSD student pays.

So what does a college really cost you, and what do you get back? We plotted 1,682 four-year schools from the U.S. Department of Education College Scorecard to find out.

Prototype writeup

What have we done so far?

We picked the College Scorecard institution-level file and cleaned it down to 1,682 schools that mainly award bachelor's degrees and report both cost and earnings. We kept nine columns per school: name, state, type, sticker cost, net price after aid, median earnings 10 years after entry, median debt, graduation rate, and enrollment. We built the main D3 scatterplot of cost against earnings, colored by school type. The chart has three working interactions: a toggle between sticker price and net price with an animated transition, checkboxes to filter by school type, and a search box that highlights one school and labels it. Hovering over any dot shows a tooltip with the school's cost, net price, earnings, debt, and graduation rate. We also wrote the opening hook and added reference annotations for UCSD and Stanford.

What will be the most challenging part to design, and why?

The hardest part will be telling a clear story on top of a dense chart with 1,682 points. Dots overlap heavily in the $20K to $40K range, so we need zoom, brushing, or a binned view to keep the chart readable without hiding schools. A second challenge is the value metric. Earnings divided by net price explodes for schools with a net price near $0, so a raw ratio ranks CUNY campuses above MIT, and we need a fairer measure such as earnings minus four years of net price. We also want linked views for debt and graduation rate that update when you select schools, and keeping several D3 charts in sync takes careful state management. Finally, we have to write scroll-driven steps that guide you through the and-but-therefore arc without making you read a wall of text.

Data: U.S. Department of Education, College Scorecard, Most Recent Institution-Level Data. Earnings are median earnings of students 10 years after entry. Net price is the average annual cost after grants and scholarships.
